The great weakness of most UFO cases is the shortage of witnesses. Westall has the opposite problem, in the best possible way: on the morning of 6 April 1966, something was watched by an estimated two hundred people — students, teachers and locals — over a suburban school in Melbourne, Australia, in broad daylight. Mass hallucination is not a serious explanation for two hundred people. Something was there. What it was is the question that has never been answered to anyone's satisfaction.

##### The oval empties

It was mid-morning at Westall High School in the suburb of Clayton South. Accounts describe students out on the sports oval catching sight of a silver-grey object — most often described as a disc or saucer, roughly domed — descending over the open ground and a neighbouring paddock known as The Grange.

Word spread, classrooms emptied, and before long a large crowd — the figure usually given is around two hundred — was watching the object together. That detail is the whole point of Westall. This was not a lone motorist on a dark road. It was a daylight event with a crowd of witnesses who knew each other, compared notes on the spot, and in many cases have told the same story consistently for the rest of their lives.

##### Down among the trees

The object, witnesses said, descended behind a stand of trees in the paddock, appeared to touch down or hover very low for a short time, then rose and departed at speed. Several reported that small aircraft — light planes — appeared and seemed to circle or pursue it before it left.

Afterwards, witnesses described a circle of flattened or scorched grass at the site, and — a recurring and unsettling element of the story — a heavy official presence, with authorities discouraging students and staff from talking about what they had seen. As ever, the "hush-up" element is the hardest part to verify, resting on memory rather than record.

##### The balloon on the table

The leading conventional explanation is a **HIBAL** balloon. In the 1960s, a joint Australian–US programme flew large high-altitude balloons carrying instrument packages to sample the upper atmosphere for radioactivity, launched from not far away. A big silvery balloon-and-payload, drifting down and being recovered, is a genuine candidate — it can look strange, it descends, and its recovery could explain an official presence.

The balloon theory has real merit and may well account for part of the day. Where it strains is the behaviour: a passive balloon drifts on the wind. It does not descend, hover with apparent purpose, sit among trees and then climb away at speed while light planes chase it — which is what a large number of the Westall witnesses describe. A balloon explains an object in the sky. It explains the reported *movement* far less well.

##### The UAP Times take

Westall earns a **credibility of 3 — Unconfirmed**, and it is one of the most frustrating cases in this entire file — not because the evidence is thin, but because the witness base is so unusually strong and the physical follow-up so weak. Two hundred daylight witnesses is extraordinary. A total absence of a photograph, a landed-craft sample, or a surviving official file is, at a 1960s suburban school, sadly ordinary.

Our reading is that something real and physical was over Clayton South that morning — the witness numbers make that close to certain — and that the honest answer is a genuine "unidentified." A HIBAL balloon is the most respectable conventional candidate and probably explains some of it; the purposeful, pursued, fast-departing craft that so many careful people describe is the part it cannot quite hold. Westall is the case that proves witnesses alone, however many, can establish that *something* happened without ever establishing *what*.
