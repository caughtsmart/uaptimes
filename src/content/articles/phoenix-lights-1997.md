The single most important fact about the Phoenix Lights is that there were two of them. Almost every argument about the case — believer or sceptic — goes wrong by treating the night of 13 March 1997 as one event. It wasn't. There was an early event, around 8pm, and a later one, around 10pm, and they have completely different explanations. One is largely solved. The other is not. Keep them apart and the whole thing snaps into focus.

##### Event one: the V that crossed a state

At about 8pm, people across a huge stretch of Arizona — from the Nevada line, down through Phoenix, and on toward Tucson — began reporting an enormous V or boomerang formation of lights moving slowly and silently overhead. Witnesses described it passing between them and the stars, occluding the sky as it went, apparently a single vast structure rather than separate aircraft.

This is the genuinely interesting event, and the harder one to dismiss, because of the sheer number and spread of witnesses reporting a consistent shape over a long track. The mundane explanation on the table is a formation of high-flying aircraft — a flight of jets whose individual lights, seen from far below at night, can read as the edge of one solid craft. Some analyses of the era's video argue exactly that. But "a formation of aircraft that thousands mistook for a single silent mile-wide object" is itself a fairly remarkable claim, and it has never been pinned to a specific, confirmed flight.

##### Event two: the lights that hung over the city

Then, around 10pm, a different thing happened: a row of bright lights appeared to hang, more or less stationary, over the Phoenix area, before slowly winking out one by one. This is the footage most people remember — the static string of glowing dots. And this is the part that is, for practical purposes, solved.

The Maryland Air National Guard confirmed that A-10 aircraft had dropped high-intensity illumination flares over the Barry Goldwater Range south-west of the city that night. From Phoenix, flares hanging under parachutes and then dropping behind the Sierra Estrella mountains would look precisely like a row of lights hovering and then extinguishing in sequence. Independent analysis of the timing, positions and the way the lights vanished behind a known ridgeline supports it strongly. The 10pm lights were almost certainly flares.

##### The governor who laughed, then didn't

Arizona's then-governor, Fife Symington, initially played the whole thing for laughs, staging a press conference in which an aide was paraded in an alien costume. It was a decade later that Symington changed the tenor of the story completely: in 2007 he stated publicly that he too had seen the 8pm craft, that it was enormous and unlike any aircraft he knew, and that he considered it unexplained. A sitting governor mocking a UFO case is ordinary; that same governor, years on, saying he witnessed it and can't explain it is not — and it's a large part of why the 8pm event still carries weight.

##### The UAP Times take

The Phoenix Lights earn a **credibility of 2 — Credible** — but only if you respect the split. The 10pm hovering lights are, to our reading, flares, and treating them as the mystery does the case a disservice. The 8pm V is the real puzzle: a huge, consistent, widely-witnessed formation, vouched for years later by the state's own governor, never conclusively tied to a specific flight of aircraft.

Our honest position is that the most likely explanation for the V — a high, silent formation of conventional planes read as one craft — is plausible but unproven, and that the case is strongest not as "proof of a spaceship" but as a first-rate example of how two ordinary-to-extraordinary events, on one night, fused in public memory into a single legend. Something crossed Arizona that evening that a great many level-headed people, up to and including the governor, could not identify. That is worth taking seriously without pretending it's settled either way.
