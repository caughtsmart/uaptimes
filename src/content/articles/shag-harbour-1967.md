Shag Harbour is the case the official record cannot shake off, because the official record made it. This was not a story that circulated in a magazine and got dressed up over the decades; it began as a police report, filed the same night, when villagers and Royal Canadian Mounted Police officers watched something descend into the cold Atlantic and triggered a genuine air-sea rescue response for a crash with no aircraft missing. Canada's own government files carry it, to this day, as an unsolved case. That pedigree is rare, and it is why Shag Harbour sits near the top of our list.

##### Lights over the water

On the evening of 4 October 1967, in the tiny fishing community of Shag Harbour on the southern tip of Nova Scotia, multiple people independently saw a low string of bright lights in the sky. The lights descended toward the harbour at a shallow angle and struck the surface of the water with what several witnesses described as a bright flash and a whistling or rushing sound, then floated on the sea before sinking.

Several of the witnesses were teenagers; others were adults; and — crucially — a Royal Canadian Mounted Police officer saw the object too. Believing they had witnessed an aircraft crash into the harbour, witnesses called the RCMP detachment. Officers went to the shore, saw a pale yellowish light or foam drifting on the water where the object had gone down, and treated it as exactly what it appeared to be: a downed aircraft with possible survivors.

##### A rescue with nothing to rescue

Local fishing boats put out into the dark harbour to look for survivors or wreckage. They found a patch of yellowish foam on the surface — and nothing else. No bodies, no oil slick of the expected kind, no debris field, no sign of an aircraft.

The response escalated. The RCMP contacted the military; a check confirmed that no civilian or known military aircraft was missing. The Royal Canadian Navy became involved, and divers searched the seabed of the harbour and the waters beyond over the following days. The official search turned up no aircraft and no conventional explanation for what a police officer and a cluster of residents had watched go into the sea.

##### The paper trail

What lifts Shag Harbour above the ordinary is the documentation. The incident generated contemporaneous RCMP reports and military message traffic, filed while the event was fresh and by people with no incentive to invent a flying saucer — they were logging what they genuinely took to be a possible fatal crash.

One government message memorably recorded that the object was not a known aircraft and the sighting could not be explained. In the files it stands as one of the very few cases where a national government's own paperwork uses, in effect, the words "unidentified flying object" as a plain administrative fact. Sceptics reasonably point out that a bright fireball or re-entering debris could produce lights and a splash, and that memory of a dramatic night hardens over decades. But a meteor does not float on the water for a time before sinking, and it does not leave a foam trail that a search party rows out to inspect.

##### The UAP Times take

Shag Harbour earns a **credibility of 2 — Credible**, and it is one of the cleanest cases on the map for a simple reason: the witnesses include police, the response was official and immediate, and the documentation was created in real time by a government treating the event as a life-and-death emergency, not a curiosity. The physical residue is thin — foam, and an absence of wreckage — but the human and institutional record is unusually solid.

Our reading is that something real came down into the water off Shag Harbour that night, was seen by sober and independent witnesses including a Mountie, and was searched for by professionals who found nothing to match it. Whether it was an exotic craft, an unlogged aircraft, or a natural object behaving unusually is not resolved and may never be. But as a demonstration that a UFO report can be a matter of official record — filed, searched, and left open by a government rather than a believer — Shag Harbour is close to unmatched.
