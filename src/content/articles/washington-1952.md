There is a particular kind of UFO case that officials cannot simply ignore, because it happens directly over their heads. In July 1952, unidentified objects were tracked on radar in the restricted airspace over Washington D.C. — not once but across two consecutive weekends — with jets scrambled, pilots reporting lights, and the story splashed across the world's front pages. It forced the US Air Force into its biggest press conference since the Second World War. And the answer it gave then is still the answer today: a trick of the weather. Whether you believe that is the whole story is the interesting part.

##### Blips over the White House

Late on the night of 19–20 July 1952, air-traffic controllers at Washington National Airport watched a cluster of slow-moving targets appear on their radar scopes in airspace where nothing should have been. The objects loitered, then occasionally accelerated away at speeds no aircraft of the day could manage. The returns showed up independently at National, at nearby Andrews Air Force Base, and on the airport's precision-approach radar.

This is the load-bearing detail: multiple radar sets, operated by different people in different places, painting targets at the same time — and, on occasion, airline pilots and observers on the ground reporting lights where the radar said something was. It is much harder to wave away a coordinated set of returns than a single scope glitch.

##### Jets that arrived too late

The Air Force scrambled F-94 jet interceptors to run down the intruders. What followed became the signature frustration of the whole episode: as the jets arrived over the city, the objects seemed to fade from the scopes, only to return once the fighters ran low on fuel and turned for home. On at least one pass a pilot reported being briefly surrounded by lights that then simply left.

Then it happened again the following weekend, 26–27 July, with the same pattern of radar returns and scrambles. Two weekends of unexplained blips over the seat of American government, in the middle of a nationwide UFO wave, was not something the authorities could let hang.

##### The biggest press conference since the war

On 29 July 1952, the Air Force convened a packed press conference — the largest it had held since 1945. The explanation offered, associated with the era's Project Blue Book, was **temperature inversion**: layers of warm air over cooler air can bend radar beams and produce false, ground-based returns that drift and dart in exactly the disconcerting way these had, while also bending light to make stars and distant lamps shimmer like hovering craft.

It was a plausible, deflating answer, and it did the job of cooling the story down. But it did not convince everyone in the room — including some of the radar operators, who insisted the targets they saw behaved like solid, controlled objects, not the mushy, smeared returns an inversion typically produces. Even Blue Book's own Captain Edward Ruppelt was privately more equivocal than the podium suggested.

##### The UAP Times take

The Washington flap earns a **credibility of 2 — Credible**. It has the ingredients that make a case durable: multiple independent radar sets, corroborating visual reports, military interceptors launched in response, and a documented official record. It also has a specific, physically real conventional explanation in temperature inversion — one that genuinely can produce fast, erratic false radar returns on a hot, humid Washington night.

The honest verdict sits in the tension between those two facts. Inversions are real and were almost certainly responsible for *some* of what the scopes showed. Whether they account for *all* of it — including the returns that experienced operators swore behaved like craft, timed to fade whenever fighters closed in — is exactly what has never been nailed down. Seventy years on, the Washington flap remains the case where the official explanation is entirely reasonable and still doesn't feel like quite enough.
