If the modern UFO conversation has a founding document, it's the 2004 Nimitz encounter off the coast of California — the "Tic Tac." It has the ingredients that most cases lack: multiple trained witnesses, radar corroboration, and eventually official acknowledgement that the footage was real and the object unidentified.

##### What holds up

The strongest part of the case has always been the witnesses. These were experienced naval aviators describing, independently and consistently, an object with no obvious means of propulsion behaving in ways that didn't fit anything in the inventory. Add radar operators reporting objects dropping from high altitude at implausible speed, and you have corroboration across different sensor types — which is rare and valuable.

The official acknowledgement matters too. The Pentagon confirming the footage was genuine took the case out of the "grainy hoax" bin and into the "genuinely unexplained" one.

##### What's been overstated

Where the case gets shakier is in the confident physics. "It went from 80,000 feet to sea level in a second" is a thrilling claim, but the underlying radar data supporting the most extreme performance figures is far less clean than the retellings suggest. A lot of the wildest numbers come from memory and interpretation, not a clean instrument log.

Extraordinary claims lean hardest on exactly the data that's weakest here. That doesn't make the witnesses wrong — it makes the *specific superlatives* unreliable.

##### The UAP Times take

Twenty-five years on, the Tic Tac remains the best case precisely because it's the most documented, not because it's the most extreme. Treat the eyewitness accounts as strong and the record-breaking performance stats as provisional, and you've got the honest version. That version is still plenty strange.
