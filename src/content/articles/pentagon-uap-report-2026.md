The Pentagon's UAP-hunting office has published its latest tally, and the number doing the rounds is 319: that's how many new reports of unidentified anomalous phenomena the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) says it received from military and government sources this cycle. It landed in July 2026, several months later than Congress had asked for, in a document that runs to a slim fourteen pages.

That sounds like a lot of cases. It is a lot of cases. But as ever with these reports, the headline number is the least interesting thing in the file. The story is in the resolutions — what AARO explained, what it couldn't, and the single entry it very visibly failed to make go away.

Here, then, is the honest read of the FY2025 report: what the numbers actually say, why the office still can't see clearly, and why the boring conclusion remains the correct one.

##### The number that made the headlines — and the one that didn't

Start with the arithmetic, because AARO's own framing is easy to misread. The 319 figure covers reports received across the period, of which 284 fall inside the official reporting window — 2 June 2024 to 30 May 2025 — and another 35 are older incidents surfaced late and folded in. So this isn't 319 fresh things in the sky over twelve months; it's a caseload, part current, part archival.

Of that new batch, analysts closed 114. Add the 256 previously logged cases they also ran to ground in the same period, and AARO resolved 370 investigations in total this cycle — comfortably more than it took in. For an office whose critics accuse it of sitting on a growing pile, that is a resolution rate worth noting before anyone reaches for the word "cover-up".

And what did the resolved cases turn out to be? The dependable cast: balloons, birds, satellites, aircraft and small uncrewed drones, with a single rocket launch and — genuinely — one manned jet pack among them. None of the resolutions pointed to advanced foreign technology, exotic materials, or anything you'd need a new branch of physics to explain. AARO repeated, in the flat institutional prose these reports favour, that it has found no verifiable evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity, or technology, and no sign that the US government or any contractor is quietly holding recovered off-world hardware.

What keeps the subject alive is the residue. Roughly 200 of the new reports remain uncategorised — not because AARO has declared them unknowable, still less extraterrestrial, but because there simply isn't enough sensor data to say anything with confidence. That distinction is the whole ballgame, and it's the one both the true believers and the "it's all weather balloons" crowd tend to skate past.

##### "The data is rubbish" — still the central problem

If there is one theme running through every AARO report since the office stood up, it's this: the data is rubbish. Not because the witnesses are unreliable — many are trained military aviators with radar corroboration and no incentive to invent anything — but because a single-sensor radar hit, or a few seconds of gun-camera infrared shot through a moving jet's optics, is a genuinely terrible basis for identifying a fast object at unknown range.

That last phrase — unknown range — is the quiet killer. A bright blob on a targeting pod could be a small object nearby moving slowly, or a large object far away moving fast, and a single camera cannot tell you which. Without distance you cannot derive size, speed or acceleration, which is to say you cannot derive any of the "impossible" performance figures that make a clip go viral. Strip the range out and a lot of the drama goes with it.

AARO's director, Dr Jon Kosloski, has been unusually candid about this, and about the fact that not everything dissolves under scrutiny. "We do have some very anomalous objects," he told a Senate Armed Services subcommittee — while in the same breath stressing that anomalous means *unresolved*, not *alien*. It is a careful, scientist's distinction, and to his credit he keeps drawing it rather than letting the audience fill in the blank.

The office has repeatedly asked for better, purpose-built sensors pointed at the problem, and has begun standing up its own multi-sensor prototyping to detect, track and characterise anomalies properly rather than relying on whatever a fighter's targeting pod happened to catch. Someone outside government has already built exactly that kind of instrument: the Galileo Project's [triangulating observatory network](/articles/galileo-project-triangulation-distance-problem/) measures the one quantity gun-camera footage can never supply — how far away the thing actually was. Until data of that calibre reaches the official pile, the unexplained column will keep refilling itself, and every annual report will read a little like this one.

##### The satellites did it (mostly)

The most quietly instructive section of the FY2025 report concerns the ones people most want to be spacecraft: objects reported up in the space domain. There were 44 such reports — and, tellingly, not a single one came from a space-based sensor. Forty-two originated from FAA civilian pilot reports and two from US Space Command ground-based sensors.

Using three-dimensional modelling and all-source analysis, AARO concluded these sightings were consistent with reflected sunlight glinting off satellites — the same effect that has people filming Starlink "trains" and swearing they've seen a fleet in formation. It is not a glamorous answer. It is almost certainly the right one, and it's a useful reminder that the sky above us is now busy with human hardware in a way it simply wasn't when the classic UFO lore was written. A great many modern sightings are, in the most literal sense, our own reflections.

This is also, incidentally, why the arrival of tens of thousands of low-orbit satellites has coincided with a rise in "lights moving strangely" reports. The phenomenon that changed is largely orbital, not extraterrestrial — a point worth holding onto the next time a night-sky video trends.

##### The one they couldn't file: a hundred objects off Virginia

Every AARO report has its outlier, and this year's is a beauty. Buried in the maritime domain — a category with exactly one entry — sits a US Navy report describing roughly 100 airborne UAP and two apparently uncrewed surface vessels operating off the coast of Virginia.

It gets a paragraph. No date, no resolution, no photograph — just a description dry enough to be easy to miss, which is precisely why it's worth slowing down for. A hundred objects is not a lone bright light misjudged by a tired pilot; it's a mass event, near a major naval concentration, with a surface component. It is exactly the sort of thing that should have been captured by multiple sensors, and exactly the sort of thing that separately became a live Pentagon investigation reported by *DefenseScoop* in the summer of 2026 — we've pulled that thread on its own in [a hundred objects and two boats off the Virginia coast](/articles/navy-uap-swarm-virginia-coast/).

Before anyone declares first contact off Chesapeake Bay: "unresolved" here still most plausibly means drones. A swarm of uncrewed systems and a pair of autonomous boats is a description that fits a foreign — or even domestic — drone incursion at least as well as it fits anything exotic, and arguably better, given how many such incursions around military sites have made the news lately. That it remains open is a data problem and possibly a security problem. It is not, on the evidence AARO has published, an alien one. But it is the case that most deserves a proper, well-instrumented answer, and the one whose absence of detail is most frustrating.

##### What "unresolved" does and doesn't mean

It's worth being precise about that word, because it does an enormous amount of load-bearing work in these reports and gets misquoted in both directions.

When AARO calls a case unresolved, it is not saying "we investigated thoroughly and found something that breaks known physics". It is almost always saying "we did not have enough good data to reach a confident conclusion, so we're not going to pretend otherwise". Most of those cases are then moved into what the office calls its active archive — over 900 reports, across its whole caseload, now sit there for lack of sufficient scientific data — where they can be reopened the moment better information turns up.

That is genuinely how a responsible investigative body should behave. A false resolution — forcing a case into the "balloon" column just to clear it — would be worse than an honest shrug. But it also means the pile of "unexplained" cases is not a pile of little green men; it's a pile of things too poorly recorded to call either way. The number will go up and down with the quality of the sensors, not the frequency of visitors.

It's the same reason the raw sighting counts keep climbing without telling us much. As we've argued in [why commercial pilots are reporting more sightings](/articles/commercial-pilots-sightings-surge/), a great deal of the recent rise reflects a reporting system that finally makes it safe and simple to file — not a sky that suddenly filled up.

##### The sceptic's read — and why it still matters

No honest account of a UAP report is complete without the debunkers, because on the specifics they have an uncomfortably good track record. Mick West, the most prominent of them, has spent years demonstrating how the famous Navy videos yield to mundane explanations: he argues the GIMBAL object's "rotation" is glare artefact spinning on a gimballed lens, and that GOFAST — which looks like something screaming low over the ocean — is a parallax illusion, an object drifting slowly at altitude only appearing to race because the jet filming it is the thing that's moving.

You do not have to like where West lands to notice that AARO's own resolutions keep vindicating the *method*. Reflected sunlight, parallax, sensor glare, balloons, drones — these are exactly the prosaic mechanisms the sceptics kept pointing at, and the government's own analysts, with far more data than any YouTuber, keep arriving at the same place. When your debunkers and your defence department independently reach for the same explanation, the explanation is probably right.

Where the sceptics overreach is in the leap from "we can explain these particular clips" to "therefore there is nothing here at all". Kosloski's handful of genuinely anomalous objects haven't been debunked; they've been *undetermined*, which is a different and more honest state. The mature position sits between the two camps and satisfies neither: most of it is ordinary, a stubborn fraction isn't yet explained, and the way to tell the difference is better instruments — not louder arguments.

##### A late report, and a system straining to keep up

One last detail worth flagging, because it says something about the machinery rather than the mystery. This report was due under a statutory annual cycle Congress laid out, and it arrived months late. AARO's caseload, meanwhile, has swollen past 2,000 lifetime reports, the bulk of them thinly evidenced and destined for that active archive.

An office that is behind on its paperwork and buried in low-quality reports is not a conspiracy; it's an understaffed clearing house drinking from a firehose. That has consequences for oversight, which is why Congress keeps trying to get the process onto a firmer statutory footing — the effort we covered when [the House voted for a full UAP disclosure framework](/articles/house-uap-disclosure-amendment-ndaa-2027/), and the reason a [White House science council was asked to weigh in](/articles/uap-science-advisory-council-loeb/), albeit with no access to the classified files. Better law and better data are the two things that would actually move this subject forward. A slim, tardy fourteen-page report is a reminder of how far both still have to go.

##### The UAP Times take

Nothing in the FY2025 report proves anything landed on the lawn — and nothing in it debunks the whole subject either. The "it's all balloons" crowd should read the resolution list carefully and note the handful their own preferred explanations don't touch; the "disclosure is imminent" crowd should read the same list and note how thoroughly the prosaic explanations keep winning.

The honest position remains the boring one, and we'll keep taking it: a serious, under-resourced office is collecting slightly better data, slowly, and telling you plainly what it can and can't explain. Watch what it can't — the anomalous few, the hundred objects off Virginia — rather than the reassuring pile it can. And keep your expectations pinned to the sensors, because until those improve, next year's report will read a great deal like this one. We rate it a 2: the report itself is rock-solid sourcing; it's the objects inside it that keep the question open.
