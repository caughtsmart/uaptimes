Most UFO cases leave nothing behind but words. Cash–Landrum left injuries. Two women and a child came off a dark East Texas road in December 1980 and, within hours and days, developed a constellation of symptoms — burns, blistering, hair loss, nausea, weakness — that looked disturbingly like the effects of intense heat and radiation. That medical aftermath, documented by doctors, is what separates this case from the thousands of lights-in-the-sky reports. Something injured these people. The question is what.

##### A wall of fire on the road

On the night of 29 December 1980, Betty Cash, Vickie Landrum, and Landrum's seven-year-old grandson Colby were driving home through the piney woods near Huffman, north-east of Houston. On a lonely stretch of road, they encountered an enormous object hanging over the treeline: a large, diamond- or cone-shaped craft, glowing brilliantly and — most memorably — periodically belching flame downward from its base, roaring, and throwing off ferocious heat.

They stopped the car. At one point Betty Cash got out; the heat, all three said, was intense enough that the car's exterior became too hot to touch comfortably, and Vickie Landrum would later recall the dashboard softening. The event terrified them, and Colby, and it went on long enough to sear itself into all three accounts.

##### The helicopters

Then came the detail that turns the case from a strange light into something with fingerprints. As the object moved off, the witnesses said, it was surrounded and seemingly escorted by a swarm of helicopters — many of them, and specifically the large tandem-rotor, twin-blade type, which they and later investigators identified as military CH-47 Chinooks.

The helicopters matter enormously. A glowing craft alone is a mystery; a glowing craft ringed by two dozen military helicopters implies that *someone with a fleet* knew about it and was involved. It reframes the case from "unknown object" to "something the military may have been escorting, testing, or chasing" — which is exactly the thread the witnesses would later try, and fail, to pull in court.

##### The illness, and the lawsuit

In the hours and days that followed, all three fell ill. Betty Cash, who had been outside the car the longest, was worst affected: severe nausea and vomiting, blistering and reddening of the skin, hair loss, and weakness that put her in hospital. The symptoms, doctors noted, resembled radiation exposure combined with burns. Cash's health was seriously and lastingly damaged.

Convinced the object had been a government craft, the women pursued a claim against the United States government for their injuries. The case was ultimately dismissed — the courts found no evidence that the object was owned or operated by the US government, and the military denied that any such craft or the described helicopter operation was theirs. That denial is either the truth or the neatest possible escape, and the case gives you no clean way to decide which.

##### The UAP Times take

Cash–Landrum earns a **credibility of 3 — Unconfirmed**, and it is one of the most substantial cases on the map precisely because of the injuries. This is not merely a story: there are contemporaneous medical records of real people who became genuinely, seriously ill after the event, with a symptom picture consistent with heat and radiation. Add multiple consistent witnesses, a child among them, and the specific, checkable detail of military-type helicopters, and you have a case with unusual physical and testimonial substance.

Our reading is that something real, hot, and dangerous was on that road, and that the military helicopter escort — if the identification is right — points to human knowledge of it rather than to a purely alien visitation. The most tantalising possibility the case raises is not extraterrestrial at all: that the witnesses stumbled onto some secret craft or accident that the government then declined, in court and out, to acknowledge. Whether exotic or terrestrial, Cash–Landrum stands as perhaps the strongest UFO case ever to produce documented human injury — and the government's flat denial, in the face of three hurt civilians, is the part that has never sat right.
